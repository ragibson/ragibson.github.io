I recently completed the Advanced Risk and Portfolio Management (ARPM) certification for quants. It requires a significant time investment, so I wanted to collect my thoughts about what I found useful, what parts were frustrating, and an assessment of the program overall.

Program overview #

The Advanced Risk and Portfolio Management (ARPM) certification, formerly the “ARPM Quant Marathon”, is a 10-month program covering Data Science, Machine Learning, and Quantitative Finance.

It is split across seven courses and several optional primers, pictured below with their current names.1

Course structure #

Each course is 6 weeks long and requires 80 hours of time-tracked work across the program content.

In each course, there are many methods of earning credit towards those hours:

  • 12 lectures (1 hour long, 2 per week) that grant 2 hours of credit each if attended live.
  • 6 homework sets that grant 3 hours of credit each (scaled by your grade).
  • 1 practical project that grants 12 hours of credit (scaled by your grade).
  • A large amount of written textbook content and Jupyter notebooks, which grant 1:1 credit for each minute of active review.2
  • A Q&A forum that grants 12 minutes of progress per question and 30 minutes per answer.

My experience #

In my case, I was able to listen in on roughly half of the lectures and received an average 98% grade across:

  • 41 of the 42 homework assignments.
  • 4 of the 7 practical projects (1 in Mathematical Statistics for Finance and the 3 from Quantitative Finance).
  • An optional self-driven research project and presentation.3

As such, I estimate that I spent ~300 hours reading the textbook and Python scripts to cover the remainder of the progress requirement.

What I liked #

Broad topic coverage #

The main courses in the ARPM certification cover quite a broad range of topics across statistics, causal inference, financial modeling, pricing theory, portfolio optimization, and so on.

I found this valuable for quant work to the extent that I could apply the material to my own problems.

Quants (and finance professionals in general) can become “siloed” in one area without much exposure to the broader field. In such cases, I think this is a great opportunity to get a sense of the range of quant work available across the industry.

This is also beneficial to newcomers without much background because it provides an introduction to a wide variety of concepts. With that knowledge, they could make a better decision of which areas they want to pursue, or simply enter the field with a stronger foundation.

Structured accountability #

Because the program has a consistent schedule and tracked deliverables, it provides an external incentive that is likely to be much more effective than self study.

This is a major benefit of proper coursework, especially if you’ve struggled with independent study in the past or want to replace unstructured leisure time with something more productive.

Flexibility #

Because there are so many different ways to earn the 80 hours of progress required in each course, you can theoretically choose which topics are most interesting to you and which types of content fit your learning style best.

The lack of exams is another significant advantage from a participant’s perspective. That removes a lot of possible stress, especially if you are already balancing the program with a full-time job.

Moreover, the courses themselves do not have to be completed by a specific deadline. You can work at your own pace and take lengthy breaks so long as you complete everything within the 10-month period. In fact, I think your progress rolls over into the following year’s offering if needed.

That said, I feel that you do generally have to split your progress across all possible avenues to reach the required 80 hours per course. Consider, if you:

  • Attend all 12 lectures (24 hours),
  • Complete all 6 homework assignments with a perfect score (18 hours), and
  • Complete the practical project with a perfect score (12 hours),

This still leaves 26 hours to earn from the textbook and Python content (say, ~125 pages and ~30 Jupyter notebooks).

In my experience, this required multiple rounds of rereading to actually hit the progress requirement. However, this is probably less of an issue if the content is unfamiliar to you.

Consistent notation #

Quant techniques draw on topics from a broad range of disciplines, so the notation used in published papers is often an organic mish-mash of related fields that can be a pain to digest.

As such, the ARPM team has written their content using a consistent notation throughout the program. There are some minor exceptions made for areas in which a particular symbol or syntax has become overwhelmingly standard in the industry, but these are clearly highlighted when first introduced.

I think this is genuinely helpful for newcomers to the field. It becomes easier at a glance to compare across topics, determine which quantities are random/deterministic, scalar/vector/matrix-valued, and so on.

Quality of training team #

This certification is relatively small, so the training team seems quite eager for feedback and to improve the course content. For similar reasons, the instructors are readily available to answer questions via email or on the ARPM forums.

Attilio Meucci, the founder of the program, is also clearly very knowledgeable and a great lecturer. I was able to listen in on a few of his lectures in the Mathematical Statistics for Finance course, and he presented several interesting connections between quant finance and general statistical methods that suggested a deep grasp of the material at hand.

Tradeoffs of quant certifications in general #

Limited hiring impact #

My impression is that certifications do not give you much of an edge in the quant job market. I feel that they should be pursued primarily for the knowledge itself, or perhaps to fill in resume gaps if you have no prior STEM or technical background.

Indeed, I have interviewed candidates with CFA, CQF, FRM, and other finance certifications who still had substantial gaps in the knowledge relevant to the role. These certifications should be treated as evidence of study rather than proof of ability or retained long-term knowledge.

If improving your job prospects is your primary goal, I think a degree, internship, rotation, or other feeder program is likely to be more valuable.

Cost #

Certifications tend to be quite expensive, so I would generally not recommend them unless they are highly relevant to (or required for) your target role, or your employer is willing to sponsor it.

  • The two levels of the full CQF program costs $23,000 - $25,000.
  • The ARPM program costs $11,500 (or $1,400 for just the lab materials for one year).4

Exam-based certifications also require paying for exams themselves, along with whatever study materials you choose to use.

  • The three CFA exams cost $3,500 - $4,600.
  • The two FRM exams cost $2,000.

It is also worth considering the opportunity cost. For example, you could instead spend the 500-600 hours on open-source work, independent research, or even moonlighting with a second job. Those options may provide a more substantial boost to your resume and possibly extra income.

Time commitment #

The time required to complete certifications is significant, and you will have to make large changes to your schedule and personal life accordingly.

The quant certifications I have looked at generally seem to target 10-15 hours per week of study time over 6-18 months. This is half of the average weekly leisure time of full-time workers in the U.S.5

In the case of ARPM, the workload is also not consistent throughout the program. It is probably closer to 13 hours/week when taking only one course, and around 18 hours/week for the ~3 months when the Mathematical Statistics for Finance course forces you to take two courses simultaneously.

To give you a sense of what that means in practice, I naturally settled on:

  • Cutting my social life roughly in half,
  • Halting the majority of my hobby projects,
  • Eliminating virtually all regular workouts, and
  • Stopping regular meal prep and most cooking at home.

ARPM-specific tradeoffs #

Limited recognition #

The ARPM certification is relatively uncommon, and only ~250 people have attained it according to the official certification page. I would therefore not expect most HR departments or hiring managers to recognize it.

It is also unfortunately a bit difficult to distinguish on LinkedIn since other, substantially shorter programs are also presented as certifications from ARPM.

  • There is a parallel, heavily compressed “ARPM Bootcamp” that’s ~20x as popular (~5,000 graduates) but requires only ~1/15th of the work.

    That one covers the same broad subject areas in ~40 hours over the course of one week rather than ~600 hours over 10 months.

  • Graduates are encouraged to post “statements of completion” from each of the seven courses on LinkedIn as they progress through the content. I have seen a few profiles where participants completed only one or two courses and then listed those as certifications.

Both of these naturally appear as “certifications from ARPM”, which likely weakens the appearance of the full ARPM certification to unfamiliar audiences.

Changing requirements #

In my case, the requirements for the certification changed a few times during the program.

The changes were not all negative, but in hindsight, the frequency and scale made it difficult to know exactly what I was committing to at the beginning of the program.

  • Time watching recorded lectures used to count towards your progress but no longer does, “to promote live classroom attendance”. I had settled into a habit of rewatching lectures to better learn the material, which was also why I didn’t have to complete the practical projects for the first 3 courses.

    As such, if you cannot make the live lectures (which occur during the workday), watching the recording provides no progress credit.

    The instructors explained that individuals who were interested in the content will still make time to watch the lectures. I understand the motivation, but would have personally preferred them to remain part of the progress mechanism to accommodate participants who could not attend live.

  • We initially had access to ~15-minute “overview lectures” from the ARPM Bootcamp that covered each unit at a high level. I found these extremely useful, but they were similarly removed after the instructors noticed that “participants relied on them rather than fully engaging with the core lectures and hands-on work”.

    I think these shorter summaries were a useful addition to the main lectures and would have been valuable to keep.

  • Practical projects were added to the program during my first or second course.

    These are designed to be significantly larger than the usual coding homework assignments, cover most of the topics from the course, and use real market data. As such, they grant 4x the progress of a typical homework set.

    I thought this was a clear improvement. I think there is enormous benefit in working through a research assignment end-to-end rather than only completing smaller, more focused homework exercises.

  • Optional homework assignments were removed early in the program. Initially, each homework assignment was longer, but only a couple were mandatory and the rest were optional for extra credit.6

    Later, all assignments became mandatory but each was supposed to be shorter than before. In practice, this essentially doubled the number of required homework assignments, and I did not find the length was reduced enough to offset that change.

    Near the end of the program, I noticed that you could skip one of the homework assignments if the rest summed up to more than 100 points, but I only discovered this by accident. This was probably mentioned in a lecture that I could not attend, and I did not watch the recordings because they no longer granted progress credit.

  • Certification projects were made optional, provided you completed the seventh Mathematical Statistics for Finance course. These were fully self-driven projects where you independently researched a topic and made a video presentation with your results.

    These projects were the most substantial independent knowledge check in a program that has no exams, so I would have personally preferred to keep some form of required independent project.

    This was also the only change that I was grandfathered out of, but I opted to take the extra seventh course anyway rather than a second research project.7

Most of these changes increased the workload, which was frustrating after I had already committed to the original expectations. I think the program would benefit from keeping the requirements fixed for the duration of a cohort, even if those requirements are changed for the future.

This ultimately contributed to burnout for me, and I am unsure if earlier or later offerings required the same rigor or workload.

EDIT (September 2026): I’ve been told the program has changed again in the 2026-2027 offering with each course scored as 40% live classroom attendance, 60% practical project, with a minimum score of 75% for each course. However, it sounds like there might still be homework assignments, so I’m not sure how this works in practice.

Textbook design and terminology #

Quant finance is not a linear learning journey, so the textbook is nonlinear, with frequent references back to earlier sections and forward to material that has not yet been covered.

I found this very distracting initially, especially when references pointed to topics I had technically not yet learned.

For example, I checked a random page and found the sample below. There are more than 20 cross-references in three paragraphs! Several point more than a dozen chapters before or after the current section.8

I eventually learned to ignore these cross-references almost entirely since parsing them properly created too much mental load.

The textbook is also very much a living document. Assigned sections and the content itself changed a few times with no notification, which I only happened to catch because I revisited older material later.

The textbook also leans more academic and some of their terminology differs from standard usage. For example, they reference the “Ricatti root” of a matrix as the true square root satisfying \(S^2 = A\) rather than involving transposes. This terminology has less than 100 results on Google and was silently updated in the textbook in the last few months to “symmetric root” and “principal root”.

There are similar phrases such as “maximum Sharpe ratio stochastic discount factor” and “minimum torsion bets” that are probably unfamiliar to practitioners outside the program.

I would also be somewhat cautious when the textbook cites Meucci’s own papers. They are academically interesting and clearly relevant to the material, but they are naturally integrated into the course content without always making it explicit where the approach differs from common industry practice.

Flexibility of assessment #

As someone who interviews candidates, the flexibility between participants is a potential downside.

In theory, one participant could:

  1. Complete multiple independent research projects, ~40 homework assignments, 7 practical coding projects, actively engage in ~80 lectures, and read much of the textbook, or
  2. Spend 560 hours reading textbook content and scripts with little other engagement with the material.

Those are very different experiences resulting in the same certification. As a result, I would be cautious about placing much weight on the credential by itself.

That said, no certification is truly representative of an individual’s knowledge. I would be more interested in seeing the candidate’s actual work, such as projects on GitHub, a personal website, or LinkedIn.

Simplicity of coding assignments #

Compared with the written homework, the coding assignments are generally very straightforward. Most can be completed largely by copying chunks from the example scripts, which tend to use the same variable names as the assignments.

I suspect this may be intentional to make the exercises accessible to participants without much coding experience. However, the tradeoff is that many of the assignments can be completed without requiring an independent implementation or demonstrating much understanding of the code.

In practice, I also think most of the assignments are within the capabilities of moderately powerful AI tools, especially if the example scripts were provided alongside the assignment.

Combined with the relative simplicity of the coding components, I feel the certification measures relatively little practical coding skill.

What I would change #

I think most of the issues I encountered could be addressed without fundamentally changing the program.

I provided most of this feedback to the training team, and I would prioritize:

  • Keep the certification requirements fixed for the duration of a cohort.
  • Keep a meaningful independent research or capstone project as part of the certification requirements.
  • Add back summary videos for each unit.
  • Provide better navigation aids for the textbook’s nonlinear structure rather than overrelying on cross-references.
  • Make textbook changes more visible to participants.
  • Increase the complexity of the coding assignments to bring them in line with the written homework.
  • Make it easier to distinguish the full certification from the shorter ARPM programs on LinkedIn.

Overall assessment #

Overall, I found the program primarily valuable as a learning experience rather than a career-advancing credential.

I have already used ideas from the certification in my day-to-day work, and I am generally glad that I took it. At the same time, the workload was substantial enough to require significant sacrifices elsewhere in my life, and I do not expect it to provide much value in hiring.

In the end, I’d recommend ARPM as a learning opportunity, particularly for someone with a significant amount of free time or looking for a structured way to study quantitative finance.

I would be much less likely to recommend it for career advancement. If your primary goal is to demonstrate your ability to employers, the same time may be better spent building visible personal projects, conducting independent research, or pursuing a degree or other feeder program instead.

In fact, for someone primarily interested in the material itself, I think there is a reasonable argument for working through the textbook resources without considering the progress requirement. You could even use the time savings to work on other projects that would provide more direct evidence of your abilities.
